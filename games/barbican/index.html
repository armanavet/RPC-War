Barbican
  • The two sides are not playing the same game. The besieger has nineteen men, engines and no time. The garrison has nine, a wall to stand on, and only has to still be there at the end.
  • The besieger wins by standing in the keep — with a man, and holding it through a reply. Not by surrounding it, not by out-shooting it from the fields. He has to get inside.
  • The garrison wins by lasting. When the relief column arrives the siege is over, however much of the wall is left standing.
  • Only the garrison may stand on the wall, where it sees further and is very hard to shift. The besieger gets up there by scaling it beside a ladder party, or by walking through a hole.
  • Engines break masonry. The ram batters the gate, the miner undermines a wall he is touching, and trebuchets bombard anything three to seven squares away. Broken wall becomes rubble, and rubble is a road into the courtyard.
  • The camp kills. Every eighth turn the besieger loses a man to nothing at all — which is what actually ended most sieges, and is the clock the garrison is playing for.
